Grounding is the whole trick

The single difference between a useful draft and a confident, wrong one.

An ungrounded model answers from everything it has ever read. Ask it for a Class 8 lesson on pressure and it will happily give you a good lesson — for the wrong syllabus, at the wrong depth, citing a textbook you don't use.

A grounded model — NotebookLM with your chapter, your syllabus, last year's question paper uploaded — answers from those pages and tells you when the answer isn't in them. Toggle the source below and watch where the answers come from.

On-syllabus: —

Same question, same model. The only change is what it is allowed to read.

What ungrounded gets wrong

Depth drift. It reaches for fluid dynamics and Pascal's law derivations that sit three years above Class 8, because most of the internet's writing on pressure is written for older students.

What grounding fixes

It answers inside your six subtopics — force, effects of force, contact and non-contact, pressure, pressure in liquids and gases, atmospheric pressure — and says "not in the material" when you stray outside them.

Practical version: upload the chapter, the syllabus extract and last year's paper to a notebook once, at the start of term. Everything for the next twelve weeks is drafted from that notebook. Ten minutes spent once removes the most common failure for the rest of the year.

The distractor trap

Where AI-written assessment quietly fails, and how to catch it in ten seconds.

Ask for a multiple-choice question and you will get four options: one right, three wrong. They will look fine. The problem is that a good wrong answer is not merely wrong — it is the specific mistake a real Class 8 child makes, so that choosing it tells you what to reteach.

Click each option below to see which misconception it catches. Two of these are diagnostic. Two are filler.

Click an option to test it

The question: "A woman in stiletto heels sinks into soft ground more than an elephant does. Why?"

The fix takes one line

"Rewrite the three wrong options so each one matches a known Class 8 misconception, and tell me which misconception each maps to." Now the quiz is a diagnostic instrument rather than a scoring device.

Why a teacher must still check

The four documented misconceptions here — force and pressure treated as interchangeable, area ignored, liquids and gases assumed identical, atmospheric pressure assumed constant with altitude — come from years of marking. A model will produce plausible distractors. It will not reliably produce your class's distractors.

From plan to artefact

A worked example of teaching visually — light as an electromagnetic wave.

Everything so far has been about planning faster. But a plan is not a lesson. What actually shifts understanding is often a picture the student can move themselves — and building that is a separate craft from writing the plan.

Here is the method applied to a topic that is genuinely hard to picture, and where the standard textbook diagram quietly creates the very misconception it is meant to remove.

λ = —

Two fields, one wave, at right angles and in step. Drag the slider through the whole spectrum.

Misconception 1 — "something is wiggling"

The textbook picture looks like a rope. Students conclude that light is a displacement of stuff, and then cannot explain how sunlight crosses empty space. Press "show what students picture" and the difference becomes arguable rather than assertable.

Misconception 2 — "two waves travelling together"

Drawing E and B in different colours invites the idea that they are separate things that happen to be side by side. Turn the magnetic field off and on: the point is that neither can exist without the other — a changing electric field creates the magnetic one, and vice versa.

Misconception 3 — "the amplitude is a height"

The vertical axis is field strength in volts per metre, not distance in metres. Nothing is physically that tall. This is the one that survives all the way to university.

What this picture is lying about

The wavelength on screen barely changes while the number underneath changes by fifteen orders of magnitude. A gamma ray and an FM radio wave cannot share a diagram honestly. Say that out loud to the class — the admission teaches more than the diagram.

The method, in four moves. One — show it moving before you name anything, so curiosity arrives before vocabulary. Two — give one control, not five; a slider with a single meaning is a thought, a dashboard is a toy. Three — make the misconception visible and let them break it themselves, because a belief a student disproves stays disproved. Four — say what the picture is lying about, every time. Every model is wrong somewhere, and naming where is the difference between teaching physics and teaching a diagram.
Why this is in a notebook about AI. A model will draft the lesson plan for this topic in about forty seconds, and the draft will be reasonable. It will not build the thing above. Deciding that this concept needs a movable picture rather than another paragraph — and knowing which wrong belief to aim it at — is the judgement the plan is built around. That is the part worth training teachers in.

Verdicts

Six honest claims about AI lesson planning, graded.

I grade things three ways: HOLDS UP PART TRUE BREAKS. Anything a workshop tells you that is all green is selling you something.

VerdictClaim
HOLDS UPIt drafts structure fast.Lesson skeletons, hooks, rubrics, question banks and five differentiated variants of the same worksheet. This is genuine, repeatable time back — the largest single win available to a teacher today.
HOLDS UPGrounding on your own material removes most factual risk.Uploading the chapter and syllabus once at the start of term is the highest-leverage ten minutes in this whole workflow.
PART TRUEIt writes good assessments.The stems are usually fine. The distractors are usually not diagnostic unless you explicitly ask for misconception-mapped options — and even then, verify against what your class actually gets wrong.
PART TRUEIt saves three hours a week.It saved 119 minutes in the worked example above — just under two hours, and only after a new 14-minute cost appeared: checking and correcting what the model got wrong. The claim is directionally right and routinely overstated. Expect the first fortnight to save nothing at all while you learn the grounding step.
BREAKSIt knows your syllabus.Ungrounded, it drifts to whichever curriculum dominates its training data and pitches two to three years above Class 8. Confident, fluent, and wrong about depth — the hardest error for a busy teacher to spot.
BREAKSIt can decide what a particular child needs.It cannot see the fortnight Aarav missed, the fight at lunch, or the fact that the class went flat last Tuesday. Every genuinely pedagogical judgement remains yours. This is not a limitation to be engineered away; it is the job.
8

What stays human

The one idea to keep.

The interesting question was never "can AI write a lesson plan". It obviously can. The question is which parts of teaching were always mechanical, and which only looked mechanical because they were tedious.

Formatting a worksheet three ways was always mechanical. Knowing which child gets which one never was. A workshop that teaches teachers the tools without teaching them that line ends the same way every time: enthusiasm for a week, then nothing changes.

So the training I would build is not a tool tour. It is one grounded notebook, one real week of that teacher's own planning, rebuilt live in the session — and an honest map of the three places it breaks, so nobody discovers those on a Monday morning in front of thirty children.

Automate the predictable

Drafting, formatting, variants, first-pass rubrics, hooks, question banks.

Never automate the trust

Which child, which tier, what to cut, when to stop and re-teach, and who needs to be asked how they are before any of it matters.
